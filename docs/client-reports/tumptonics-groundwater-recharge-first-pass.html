What the mapping can tell us now

The LiDAR DTM can show slope, local hollows, drainage lines and likely surface-water movement. Those features help choose where to inspect for wet ground, springs, seepage, drains, clay layers, permeable bands or other evidence that water is moving toward the spring.

Groundwater recharge is more complex than surface flow. A recharge area may sit upslope of the spring, but its shape depends on geology, superficial deposits, fractures, permeability, drainage, field drains and local water levels. This map should therefore be treated as a fieldwork guide and hypothesis, not as a final catchment boundary.

Suggested field checks

  • Walk the candidate upslope area after rainfall and photograph wet flushes, drains, seepage lines and any iron staining.
  • Check whether water appears at the spring soon after rainfall, or whether the response is delayed.
  • Look for field drains, old ditches, pipe outlets, clay/sand boundaries and visible changes in vegetation.
  • If the question becomes material to design or liability, ask a hydrogeologist to review the geology and consider monitoring or tracer testing.

Careful wording for the client

A recharge feature may be able to influence the spring, but we should not claim that from desk study alone. The sensible next step is to map the likely recharge investigation area using LiDAR, geology and groundwater datasets, then use field observations to decide whether more formal hydrogeological work is justified.

Limitations: This first pass is based on public mapping and local terrain analysis. It is suitable for scoping, discussion and planning field observations. It is not a substitute for a hydrogeological assessment, groundwater monitoring, abstraction data review, or tracer testing.
